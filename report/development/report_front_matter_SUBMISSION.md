## ABSTRACT

```
                             ABSTRACT

Football clubs commit substantial and largely irreversible capital to the
transfer market under conditions of imperfect information. This project
asked whether observable performance data could support a defensible
valuation framework, and whether the gap between a fundamentals-based
valuation and the market's own price could identify recruitment
opportunities a club could act on. That question was treated as a
hypothesis to be tested rather than a premise to be assumed.

Using 15,925 player-seasons across five major European leagues from
2015/16 to 2024/25, a valuation model combining performance, age, position,
league and career trajectory achieved an R-squared of 0.678 on a held-out
season in a season-normalised framework, against 0.383 for a context-only
benchmark. Observable football carries substantial valuation signal.
However, the study did not find sufficient out-of-sample evidence to
support the hypothesis that the resulting valuation residual represents
systematically exploitable market mispricing: two pre-specified signal
definitions were tested under strict time-based discipline and neither
produced a significant subsequent appreciation advantage.

The same residual instead proved strongly associated with subsequent exit
from top-five football. Flagged players left at 3.7 times the benchmark
rate, and a dedicated exit-risk model achieved an out-of-sample AUC of
0.732 with strong calibration. The system was accordingly redesigned around
this evidence: a risk-aware constrained optimizer allocates a transfer
budget on position-appropriate quality, development potential and value
efficiency, penalised by validated exit risk and model uncertainty.
Explainability separates why the model produced a valuation from why the
optimizer selected a player, and a guarded generative-AI layer converts
validated outputs into committee briefs subject to automated fidelity
checks.

The contribution is the evidence-driven transition from player valuation to
risk-aware recruitment decision support. The residual proved to be a
warning signal rather than an arbitrage signal: a model's disagreement with
the market is shown to be a question warranting investigation rather than
an opportunity to exploit, and the resulting system is positioned as a
screening and investigation tool rather than an autonomous decision-maker.

Keywords: player valuation, transfer market, explainable AI, SHAP,
constrained optimisation, decision support, market efficiency, sports
analytics
```

## APPENDIX F — STATEMENT ON THE USE OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

```
Artificial intelligence was used substantially throughout this project, and
its role is set out here in full.

SCOPE OF USE

AI was used for code authorship across the entire analytical pipeline: data
assembly, feature engineering, modelling, back-testing, exit-risk
modelling, constrained optimisation, explainability analysis, and the
production of figures, the decision workbook and the interactive dashboard.
It was also used to draft report prose, which was subsequently reviewed and
revised.

AI further participated in methodological discussion. Candidate approaches
including the choice of domain and dataset, the nested three-model
comparison, the time-consistency architecture, the reformulation of the
optimizer objective, the exit-risk component, and the design of the
explainability layer were proposed, critiqued and refined through
iterative dialogue between the author and AI systems. Claiming otherwise
would misrepresent how the work was produced.

DIVISION OF AUTHORITY

The author retained decision authority throughout. Every proposed approach
was accepted, modified or rejected by the author, and several were rejected
outright. The author directed the treatment of market mispricing as a
falsifiable hypothesis rather than a working assumption; required the
testing procedure to be specified in writing before any outcome was
examined; ruled that the investigation stop after two pre-specified signal
definitions returned null results rather than continue searching for a
significant specification; required the original optimizer objective to be
withdrawn once the back-test failed to support it; and determined the final
framing of the negative result as the project's central finding.

The author is responsible for the final research design, the
interpretations, and the conclusions presented in this report.

VERIFICATION

A formal project-control process was maintained requiring every numerical
claim appearing in the report, workbook, dashboard, presentation and
generated briefs to be traced to the artifact file that produced it, rather
than to any intermediate description. Under this process several
implementation and reporting defects were identified and corrected before
finalisation, including a data-leakage fault in the optimizer's uncertainty
estimate, a misreported estimator name, and a statistical measure reported
in an incorrect form. The full audit record is provided in Appendix G.

GENERATIVE AI AS AN OBJECT OF STUDY

A distinction should be drawn between the two roles generative AI plays in
this work. As described above, AI was used as a production aid throughout:
writing code, producing figures and drafting prose.

Separately, the generative-AI recruitment-brief component described in
Section 6 is itself an object of study. It converts validated analytical outputs into
recruitment-committee briefs under automated fidelity checks, performs no
analysis of its own, and its outputs are evaluated as a research result
rather than used to author this report.
```
